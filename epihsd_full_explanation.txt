EpiHSD FULL EXPLANATION
Epidemic Hierarchical Sparse Dynamics
A Complete Account of the Method, Code, Dataset, Motivation, and Results
================================================================================


================================================================================
PART 1. THE DATASET
================================================================================

1.1 What the dataset is

The dataset is a longitudinal epidemiological record of dengue fever incidence
across all 32 provinces of the Dominican Republic. It spans the years 2015
through 2023, and it is organized at weekly temporal resolution. Each row in
the dataset corresponds to one province in one week of one year, giving the
dataset a panel structure where time, geography, and a set of covariates
intersect. The raw file is called extended_input_normalized.csv and contains
roughly 11,000 rows after expansion to weekly granularity across all provinces.

1.2 The target variable

The quantity being predicted is incidence, which here means the number of
confirmed or suspected dengue cases reported per 100,000 population in a given
province in a given week. This is the central epidemiological outcome of
interest. Dengue fever is a mosquito-borne viral disease transmitted primarily
by the Aedes aegypti mosquito. In the Dominican Republic it is endemic,
meaning it circulates continuously year-round at varying intensities rather
than arriving as discrete epidemic waves with long inter-epidemic silence.

1.3 The covariate features

The input columns include meteorological variables such as rainfall, temperature,
relative humidity, and heat index. They include environmental indices related
to vegetation cover, land surface temperature, and normalized difference
vegetation index computed from satellite imagery. They include lagged incidence
values computed over several prior weeks. They include time features encoding
the seasonal position within the calendar year. After adding two engineered
temporal features called time_sin and time_cos, the total input dimensionality
is 33 features per observation.

The meteorological and environmental variables matter because dengue
transmission is deeply climate-coupled. Aedes aegypti breeds in standing water,
so rainfall drives mosquito population expansion with a delay of roughly two to
four weeks. Temperature governs the extrinsic incubation period, which is the
time from when a mosquito ingests a blood meal containing virus to when it
becomes infectious. Higher temperatures accelerate viral replication inside the
mosquito, shortening this incubation period from roughly 14 days at 25 degrees
Celsius to roughly 7 days at 30 degrees Celsius. Humidity affects mosquito
survival and biting frequency. NDVI, the vegetation index, correlates with
the availability of resting habitat for adult mosquitoes and with the presence
of water-retaining plant structures that serve as breeding sites.

1.4 The temporal structure

The data is split into three non-overlapping temporal partitions. The training
partition covers years 2015 through 2019, giving five calendar years of weekly
data. The validation partition covers years 2020 and 2021. The test partition
covers years 2022 and 2023. This split was designed so that model development,
hyperparameter tuning, architecture selection, and equation discovery all use
only training and validation data. The test partition is treated as a completely
locked holdout, never observed during any stage of development, and evaluated
exactly once at the end of the pipeline. This prevents any form of test-set
overfitting or unconscious data snooping.

1.5 The spatial structure

The 32 provinces of the Dominican Republic span the entire island territory
except for the Haitian side of Hispaniola. They range enormously in size,
population density, urbanization level, and epidemiological character. Distrito
Nacional and Santo Domingo are the capital region provinces with very high
population density, extensive urban infrastructure, and correspondingly high
absolute case counts. Provinces such as Azua, San Juan, and Pedernales are
rural, inland, and geographically isolated with much lower population densities
and substantially different transmission dynamics. Several provinces along the
western border, including Dajabón, Elías Piña, Independencia, and Baoruco,
are directly adjacent to the Haitian border and experience significant
cross-border population movement, which creates disease importation pressure
that is completely absent in interior provinces.

This spatial heterogeneity is the single most important challenge in modeling
this dataset. A model that works well for Santo Domingo may fail completely
for Azua not because the physics of dengue transmission are different but
because the unmeasured structural factors, healthcare access, reporting
completeness, vector control coverage, urban versus rural mosquito habitat
density, human mobility patterns, and socioeconomic determinants of exposure,
differ dramatically across provinces. These unmeasured factors are referred to
throughout this work as ghost variables.

1.6 The seasonality structure

Dengue in the Dominican Republic follows a bimodal seasonal pattern with
two peaks per year, one in late spring to early summer and a larger one in
late summer to autumn. This pattern is driven by the interaction of rainfall
seasonality and temperature seasonality. The time features time_sin and
time_cos encode the position within the 52-week calendar year as a continuous
circular variable, providing the model with a smooth representation of seasonal
forcing without artificial discontinuities at year boundaries.

1.7 The latent representation

Rather than working directly with the 33-dimensional raw feature space, the
pipeline first compresses each weekly observation through a trained autoencoder
neural network. The autoencoder is a deep feedforward network with encoder
layers of dimensions 512 and 256 before projecting to a 6-dimensional latent
space, and a mirrored decoder that reconstructs the 33 original features from
those 6 latent dimensions. The encoder uses LeakyReLU activations, batch
normalization, and dropout at rate 0.158. It is trained by minimizing
mean squared reconstruction error on the training years only.

The resulting 6-dimensional latent vectors z1 through z6 are compressed
representations of the full epidemiological state of a province in a given
week. They capture the most important modes of variation in the data. Because
the autoencoder is trained to reconstruct all 33 input features simultaneously,
each latent dimension implicitly integrates information from climate variables,
environmental indices, and incidence history into a single continuous value.
The latent space is therefore a compressed, smooth, low-dimensional manifold
of the epidemiological state space.

The choice of 6 latent dimensions was based on reconstruction quality across
a sweep from dimension 2 to dimension 9. Dimension 6 achieves a validation
reconstruction R2 of 0.867, which represents a point of diminishing returns,
capturing most of the meaningful variance without including noise-driven
dimensions.

After training, the autoencoder is used to encode all three data splits,
train, validation, and test, into their respective latent representations.
The test data is encoded using the frozen pretrained encoder without any
retraining or fine-tuning, preserving the strict temporal separation between
splits.

1.8 Summary of dataset properties relevant to algorithm design

The dataset has a small sample size relative to its spatial complexity. With
32 provinces and roughly 52 weeks per year over 5 training years, there are
roughly 8,320 training observations. This is enough for classical statistical
models but too small for end-to-end deep learning approaches that require
learning representations and dynamics jointly. The dataset has strong
autocorrelation, the single strongest predictor of next week incidence is this
week incidence. The dataset has a hierarchical structure with observations
nested within provinces nested within geographic clusters. The dataset has
unmeasured confounders that create systematic between-province differences
not explainable by the observed features. All four of these properties shaped
the design of EpiHSD directly.


================================================================================
PART 2. THE MOTIVATION
================================================================================

2.1 Why existing methods failed

Before EpiHSD was developed, four broad classes of methods were attempted on
this dataset.

The first class was classical machine learning with lag features, including
linear regression, ridge regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and
neural network regressors. The best of these, random forest with five lag
features of incidence, achieved a test R2 of 0.493. This is barely above the
persistence baseline of 0.470, which simply predicts that next week equals
this week. The ML benchmark failed because it treats all provinces as
exchangeable, ignoring the hierarchical spatial structure and ghost variable
heterogeneity. It also has no mechanism for equation discovery or
interpretability. It cannot tell us anything about the mechanistic drivers
of dengue dynamics.

The second class was SINDy, sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics. SINDy
is a method developed by Brunton, Proctor, and Kutz in 2016 for discovering
governing equations from data. It constructs a library of candidate polynomial
terms from the state variables and uses sparse regression to select which terms
appear in the true governing equation. When applied to the latent space
representations of the dengue data, SINDy produced deeply negative R2 values
around negative 1.7 on average. The fundamental reason for this failure is that
SINDy was designed for systems governed by ordinary differential equations with
polynomial right-hand sides. The dengue latent dynamics are not polynomial ODEs.
They are autoregressive stochastic processes with long-range temporal
dependencies. Using only z at time t as the feature vector gives an extremely
unfavorable data-to-parameter ratio of roughly 1.8, meaning SINDy had fewer
training samples than parameters to estimate, making the system badly
underdetermined.

The third class was GRU neural networks with province embeddings. A GRU is a
gated recurrent unit, a type of recurrent neural network designed for sequence
modeling. After Bayesian hyperparameter tuning with 60 Optuna trials, the best
GRU configuration achieved a test R2 of 0.290, worse than both the persistence
baseline and random forest. The reason is that with only 8,320 training samples
the GRU has too many free parameters to learn from scratch. End-to-end deep
sequence learning requires data on the order of hundreds of thousands of
sequences to generalize reliably. With weekly dengue data from 32 provinces over
5 years, there simply are not enough training examples.

The fourth class was cluster-level SINDy, which applied SINDy separately to
each of five province clusters. This also failed for the same fundamental
reason that latent dynamics are not ODEs, with cluster-level R2 values still
strongly negative.

2.2 The core insight motivating EpiHSD

After these failures, the following insight emerged. The data has two things
that need to be exploited simultaneously. First, it has strong temporal
autocorrelation at multiple lag scales. Second, it has a clear hierarchical
structure where provinces share a common base dynamic but deviate from it in
structured ways tied to geographic clustering. The right algorithm needed to
combine explicit lag features for autocorrelation capture, sparse regression
for interpretability and protection against overfitting, and hierarchical
residual fitting for capturing structured between-province heterogeneity.

No existing algorithm does all three simultaneously in a latent epidemiological
space. SINDy does sparsity but not lags and not hierarchy. GRU does lags but
not sparsity and not hierarchy. Hierarchical linear models do hierarchy but not
sparsity in the equation structure and not autoregressive lags of a
latent variable. This gap is exactly what EpiHSD fills.

2.3 The inspirations

SINDy contributed the core idea of sparse equation discovery via
sequentially thresholded least squares. The sparsity is essential here because
it prevents overfitting in a regime where the feature space has 25 terms but
the dynamics are likely driven by only a handful of them. It also produces
interpretable equations rather than a black-box weight matrix.

Gradient boosting contributed the residual fitting strategy. In gradient
boosting each successive model fits the residuals left by all previous models,
building up a strong predictor incrementally. EpiHSD applies this same
principle hierarchically: the global model explains the common variance, and
each cluster model fits only what the global model could not explain, and each
province model fits only what the cluster model could not explain. This
reduces the effective learning problem at each level to just the unexplained
variance, keeping the problem tractable with small data.

Hierarchical linear models, also known as mixed effects models, contributed
the conceptual framework of a shared population-level effect with group-level
deviations. In a hierarchical linear model you estimate a fixed effect that
applies to all units and a random effect for each group that captures
group-specific deviations. EpiHSD implements this but replaces the random
effects with sparse residual equations discovered by STLSQ, giving
interpretable rather than just numerical deviations.

Vector autoregression contributed the idea of using multiple lagged values of
a multivariate state vector as joint predictors. In a VAR model the state at
time t is predicted from the states at times t-1, t-2, and so on up to some
maximum lag. EpiHSD uses lags 0, 1, 2, and 3, covering a four-week window,
which corresponds to roughly the combined duration of the dengue extrinsic
incubation period and the intrinsic incubation period in the human host.


================================================================================
PART 3. THE METHOD IN FULL DETAIL
================================================================================

3.1 Overview

EpiHSD stands for Epidemic Hierarchical Sparse Dynamics. It is a three-level
residual sparse autoregressive framework for discovering interpretable
governing equations of epidemic dynamics in learned latent space. The three
levels correspond to global, cluster, and province. At each level, the
algorithm fits a sparse polynomial autoregressive equation to the residuals
left unexplained by the previous level, then adds the new sparse equation
to the existing one. The final province-level equation is the sum of the
global equation, the cluster correction, and the province correction.

3.2 The state representation

At each time step t, the epidemiological state of province p is represented
by a vector z_t in R^6. This is the 6-dimensional latent encoding produced
by the trained autoencoder. The goal of EpiHSD is to discover a function f
such that z_{t+1} is approximately equal to f applied to z_t, z_{t-1}, z_{t-2},
and z_{t-3}. That is, the function takes the current state and the three most
recent prior states and predicts the next state. This is a one-step-ahead
prediction in the latent space.

3.3 Feature construction

For a sequence of T latent vectors belonging to a single province, the feature
matrix Theta is constructed as follows. For each time step t ranging from
N_LAGS to T-2, a feature vector is built by concatenating z_t, z_{t-1},
z_{t-2}, and z_{t-3}. This gives a raw feature vector of dimension 6 times 4
equals 24. With a polynomial degree of 1, the PolynomialFeatures transformer
from scikit-learn adds a constant bias term, giving 25 features total. The
target for each row is z_{t+1}, the latent state at the next time step.

The constant bias term is important. In a system with no bias, the zero state
maps to the zero next state. For a disease system, the constant term in the
equation captures the autonomous level of the process, the baseline around
which dynamics evolve. When the bias term is nonzero in a cluster-level delta
equation, it means that cluster has a systematic shift in its latent dynamics
that cannot be explained by any combination of its own recent state, only by
an external forcing term not in the dataset. This is the mathematical signature
of a ghost variable.

Before feature construction, all latent vectors are standardized using a
StandardScaler fitted on the training years only. This prevents the regression
from being influenced by the absolute scale of individual latent dimensions
and ensures that the threshold in STLSQ is applied in a scale-invariant way.

3.4 Sequentially Thresholded Least Squares

STLSQ is the sparse regression algorithm at the heart of EpiHSD. It operates
on a feature matrix Theta of dimension n by p and a target matrix Y of
dimension n by K, where n is the number of training pairs, p is the number
of polynomial terms, and K is the number of latent dimensions being predicted.
It returns a coefficient matrix W of dimension p by K.

The algorithm begins by computing the ordinary least squares solution
W equal to the pseudoinverse of Theta times Y. It then enters an iterative
thresholding loop. In each iteration, any coefficient in W whose absolute value
is smaller than the threshold parameter is set to zero. Then, for each output
dimension k separately, a new least squares fit is computed using only the
features whose coefficients for that dimension are still nonzero. This refits
the active coefficients without the interference of the zeroed-out features,
which can slightly increase the magnitude of the active coefficients and push
some of them above the threshold. The loop runs for a fixed number of iterations
set to 30.

The effect of STLSQ is that the final coefficient matrix W is sparse: most
entries are exactly zero. The threshold parameter controls the degree of
sparsity. A smaller threshold retains more terms and produces a richer equation
but risks overfitting. A larger threshold produces a sparser equation but risks
underfitting. In EpiHSD, the global and cluster levels use a threshold of 0.05
and the province level uses a slightly higher threshold of 0.08 to prevent
overfitting at the most granular level where the fewest training samples are
available per unit.

3.5 Level 1. The global equation

The global equation is fitted on all 32 provinces combined over the training
years 2015 through 2019. For each province, the training latent sequence is
extracted, standardized, and used to build lag feature rows as described above.
All rows from all provinces are stacked into a single large feature matrix
Theta_global of dimension 8192 by 25 and a target matrix Y_global of dimension
8192 by 6. The 8192 rows come from roughly 256 weeks of training data per
province times 32 provinces, minus the N_LAGS rows lost at the beginning of
each province sequence.

STLSQ is applied to Theta_global and Y_global with the global threshold of 0.05,
producing the sparse coefficient matrix W_global of dimension 25 by 6. Each
column of W_global is the sparse equation for one latent dimension. The equation
for latent dimension zi reads as zi at t+1 equals the sum of nonzero coefficients
times their corresponding lag features.

This global equation captures the common dengue latent dynamics shared across
all 32 provinces. It is the population-average equation, the best single
equation that minimizes prediction error for the Dominican Republic as a whole.

3.6 Level 2. Cluster residual equations

After fitting the global equation, the residuals are computed for each cluster.
For cluster c, the residuals are the differences between the true training
targets Y_c and the predictions made by the global equation W_global applied
to the cluster training features Theta_c. These residuals R_c represent the
systematic deviations of cluster c from the global average that the global
equation cannot explain.

STLSQ is then applied to Theta_c and R_c with the cluster threshold of 0.05,
producing the sparse delta coefficient matrix delta_W_c of dimension 25 by 6.
The full cluster equation is then W_c equals W_global plus delta_W_c. When
making predictions for a province in cluster c, the combined equation W_c
is used instead of W_global.

The delta_W_c matrix is the mathematical representation of how cluster c
deviates from the global average. Its nonzero entries are the ghost variable
signatures. A nonzero constant term in delta_W_c for a given latent dimension
means cluster c has a systematic level offset in that dimension that persists
regardless of the current state. A nonzero lag coefficient in delta_W_c for a
feature that has no corresponding global coefficient means cluster c has an
active dynamic coupling between those latent dimensions that other provinces
do not have.

3.7 Level 3. Province residual equations

After fitting the cluster equations, province-level residuals are computed
for each of the 32 provinces individually. For province p in cluster c, the
residuals are the differences between the true training targets for province p
and the predictions made by the cluster equation W_c. These residuals represent
the idiosyncratic deviations of province p from its cluster, the fine-grained
local factors specific to that single province.

STLSQ is applied to the province residuals with the province threshold of 0.08,
producing delta_W_p. The full province equation is W_p equals W_c plus delta_W_p.
If a province has too few training samples to fit a province residual regression
reliably, its equation defaults to the cluster equation with no province
correction.

3.8 Prediction and evaluation

At evaluation time, one-step-ahead predictions are made for each province in
the validation and test years using the province-level equation W_p. For each
time step t in the evaluation period, the four lag vectors z_t through z_{t-3}
are taken from the true observed latent sequence, transformed into a feature
vector, and multiplied by W_p to produce z_hat at t+1. The prediction is
compared to the true latent vector at t+1.

The evaluation metric is the coefficient of determination R2, computed as
1 minus the sum of squared residuals divided by the total sum of squares
of the target. An R2 of 1.0 means perfect prediction. An R2 of 0.0 means
the model predicts no better than predicting the mean. A negative R2 means
the model is worse than the mean, which is catastrophic. All results are
computed separately for the validation years 2020 to 2021 and the test years
2022 to 2023, with the test results treated as the final performance estimate.


================================================================================
PART 4. THE CODE IN DETAIL
================================================================================

4.1 Imports and configuration

The script begins by importing numpy and pandas for numerical and tabular
computation, torch and torch.nn for loading the pretrained autoencoder,
sklearn.preprocessing for PolynomialFeatures and StandardScaler, and
sklearn.metrics for r2_score.

The configuration block defines the key hyperparameters. LATENT_DIM is set to
6, matching the dimensionality of the trained autoencoder. N_LAGS is set to 3,
meaning the feature vector includes the current state and the three preceding
states. POLY_DEG is set to 1, meaning no interaction terms between lag features
are created beyond the linear terms plus a bias constant. THRESH_GLOBAL and
THRESH_CLUSTER are both set to 0.05. THRESH_PROVINCE is set to 0.08. STLSQ_ITERS
is set to 30. The train, val, and test year ranges are defined as lists.

4.2 Loading the autoencoder and encoding test data

The WeeklyAE class defines the autoencoder architecture using PyTorch. The
constructor builds the encoder and decoder as nn.Sequential chains of linear
layers, batch normalization layers, activation functions, and dropout layers.
The encoder ends with a linear layer to the latent dimension with no activation,
allowing the latent values to take any real value. The decoder ends with a
Sigmoid activation to constrain the reconstruction to the range 0,1, consistent
with the normalized input data.

The pretrained model is loaded from disk with torch.load using weights_only=True
for security. The raw data CSV is read and sorted by province, year, and week.
Time features are added. Missing values are filled with training-year column means.
The test rows are extracted, encoded through the frozen encoder, and the resulting
latent vectors are stored in a DataFrame with province, year, and week identifiers.

The saved training and validation latent CSV is then loaded and the test latent
rows are concatenated onto it, giving a complete latent DataFrame covering all
years from 2015 through 2023. The cluster assignments are merged in from
province_clusters.csv, which was produced by the earlier K-means clustering step.

4.3 The build_lag_features function

This function takes a time series of latent vectors Z_seq of shape T by K
and returns the feature matrix Theta and the target matrix. The logic is as
follows. A list lag_vecs is built by iterating lag from 0 to N_LAGS inclusive.
For lag equal to 0, the rows taken are Z_seq from index N_LAGS to index T-2,
which are z_t values. For lag equal to 1, the rows are Z_seq from index
N_LAGS-1 to index T-3, which are z_{t-1} values. For lag equal to N_LAGS,
the rows are Z_seq from index 0 to index T-2-N_LAGS, which are z_{t-N_LAGS}.
These are concatenated horizontally to give the raw lag feature matrix.
PolynomialFeatures with degree 1 and include_bias=True then adds the constant
column.

The target is Z_seq from index N_LAGS+1 to the end, which are the z_{t+1}
values. The critical correctness requirement is that the row index of the
target must be exactly one step ahead of the lag=0 feature rows. With lag=0
features at indices N_LAGS to T-2, the targets must be at indices N_LAGS+1
to T-1, which is exactly Z_seq[N_LAGS+1:]. This alignment was verified
carefully after discovering a data leakage bug where an earlier version had
the target aligned with the lag=0 features rather than one step ahead,
producing trivially perfect R2 of 1.0.

4.4 The build_dataset function

This function loops over all provinces in a DataFrame and calls build_lag_features
on each province's training latent sequence. It stacks all resulting feature and
target rows into two large arrays Theta and Y. The function accepts an optional
scaler argument. If a scaler is provided, it is applied to the latent vectors
before feature construction, so that all features are in standardized units.

4.5 The stlsq function

This function implements sequentially thresholded least squares as described
in Part 3.4. The initial least squares solution is computed using numpy.linalg.lstsq
with rcond=None to avoid deprecation warnings. The iterative loop runs for
n_iters iterations. In each iteration, the small mask is computed as the element-wise
condition abs of W less than threshold. W is then zeroed out wherever small is True.
For each output dimension k, the active mask is the set of features still nonzero
for that dimension. If no features remain active, that output dimension is predicted
as zero. Otherwise, a new least squares fit is computed using only the active columns
of Theta against the k-th column of Y, and the result is placed back into the active
rows of the k-th column of W.

4.6 The predict_rollout function

This function evaluates a coefficient matrix W against a given set of years for
each province. It loops over provinces, extracts the latent sequence, standardizes
it, and then runs the one-step-ahead prediction loop. For each time step t from
N_LAGS to T-2, it builds the lag feature vector by concatenating z_t through
z_{t-N_LAGS}, applies the polynomial transformer, and multiplies by W to get
the predicted z_{t+1}. The predictions are collected and compared to the true
values Z_sc[N_LAGS+1:], which is the sequence of true z_{t+1} values.

R2 is computed by flattening both predicted and true matrices and calling
r2_score, treating all six latent dimensions jointly as a single prediction task.
This gives an overall assessment of how well the equation tracks the full
latent state trajectory, not just individual dimensions.

4.7 The three main execution blocks

The Level 1 block builds the global training dataset, calls stlsq on it, then
calls predict_rollout for both validation and test years using W_global.

The Level 2 block loops over the five cluster IDs. For each cluster, it extracts
the cluster subset of the latent DataFrame, builds the cluster training dataset,
computes residuals by subtracting Theta_c times W_global from Y_c, fits STLSQ
to those residuals, and forms W_c by adding delta_W_c to W_global.

The Level 3 block loops over the 32 provinces individually. For each province,
it retrieves the cluster equation W_c for that province's cluster, builds the
province training lag features, computes residuals after W_c, fits STLSQ to
those residuals, and forms W_p. Then it evaluates W_p on both the validation
and test years for that province and records the R2.

4.8 Output saving

Four output files are written. epihsd_results.csv contains one row per level
with columns for level identifier, number of units, validation R2, test R2, and
number of delta terms. epihsd_byregion.csv contains one row per province per
split with the R2 achieved by that province's equation on that split.
epihsd_equations.txt contains the human-readable equations at global and cluster
levels written out as algebraic expressions. epihsd_coefficients.csv contains
the full coefficient table in long format with columns for level, cluster, output
dimension, feature name, and coefficient value.


================================================================================
PART 5. THE RESULTS IN EXHAUSTIVE DETAIL
================================================================================

5.1 Performance versus baselines

The persistence baseline, predict next week equals this week, achieves a test
R2 of 0.470. The best ML model, random forest with lag features, achieves a
test R2 of 0.493. The GRU after hyperparameter tuning achieves a test R2 of
0.290. SINDy achieves deeply negative test R2 around negative 1.7. EpiHSD
achieves a global test R2 of 0.797. This is a 61 percentage point improvement
over random forest, and it also provides interpretable sparse equations rather
than a black box. EpiHSD also substantially outperforms the persistence baseline
by 33 percentage points.

5.2 Level 1 global equation results

The global equation achieves a mean validation R2 of 0.787 and a mean test R2
of 0.797 across all 32 provinces. The fact that the test R2 is marginally higher
than the validation R2 is unusual but not alarming given that the difference is
0.010. It suggests the 2022 to 2023 period had dengue dynamics that were not
more difficult to predict than the 2020 to 2021 period, despite the additional
two-year temporal gap from the training data.

5.3 Level 1 global equation structure

The six global equations reveal the following structure.

For z1: the dominant term is the self-lag at time t with coefficient 0.563.
z1 is also positively influenced by z3 at lag0 with coefficient 0.140 and
by z1 at lag1 with coefficient 0.169 and at lag3 with coefficient 0.085.
z2 appears as a negative influence at lag0 with coefficient negative 0.083
and as a positive influence at lag1 and lag3 with small coefficients. z5 and
z6 appear at lag3 as negative corrections. Interpretation: z1 has strong
persistence, meaning it changes slowly from week to week. Its coupling with
z3 at lag0 suggests these two dimensions represent a tightly coupled
subsystem, possibly the joint encoding of recent rainfall and resultant
mosquito abundance.

For z2: the dominant term is self-lag at time t with coefficient 0.712. This
is the strongest self-persistence of all six dimensions, indicating z2 captures
a very slowly changing factor. z1 enters negatively at lag0 with coefficient
negative 0.203, suggesting a negative coupling between z1 and z2 in the
short term. z2 also self-corrects via lag1 with coefficient 0.205. z4 enters
at lag1 with a small negative coefficient and z3 enters at lag3 with a negative
correction. Interpretation: z2 may represent a slow background climate or
seasonal state. The strong autoregression and weak cross-couplings are
consistent with a slowly varying environmental forcing factor.

For z3: the dominant term is self-lag at time t with coefficient 0.538. z2 at
lag0 drives z3 positively with coefficient 0.113. z3 then self-reinforces via
lag1 and lag2 with coefficients 0.117 and 0.118. A negative coupling from z5
at lag2 and a negative coupling from z4 at lag3 act as damping terms.
Interpretation: z3 appears to represent a medium-term dynamical component
that is driven by z2 and in turn modulates z5. This is consistent with a
rainfall to water accumulation to larval habitat to adult mosquito chain.

For z4: the dominant term is self-lag at time t with the highest coefficient
of all dimensions at 0.786. z4 has remarkably few cross-couplings compared
to other dimensions. z1 enters negatively at lag0 and lag1, z3 enters at
lag0 positively and at lag1 negatively, and z6 enters at lag2 and lag3 with
small corrections. Interpretation: z4 is the most slowly evolving latent
dimension. Its near-unit autoregressive coefficient means it barely changes
from week to week. This is consistent with a factor like long-term herd
immunity level, cumulative vector control investment, or a structural
socioeconomic index that varies on the scale of months rather than weeks.

For z5: the dominant term is self-lag at time t with coefficient 0.649. z1
enters positively at lag0 with coefficient 0.122. z5 then self-reinforces
via lag1, lag2, and lag3 with decreasing coefficients of 0.147, 0.050, and
0.089. Interpretation: z5 is a medium-persistence dimension that receives
input from z1 and decays through its own autoregression. This is consistent
with the incidence trajectory itself or a closely related transmission intensity
measure.

For z6: the dominant self-lag at time t has coefficient 0.616. z5 enters
strongly at lag0 with coefficient 0.156 but very strongly negative at lag3
with coefficient negative 0.223. This is the most distinctive coupling in
the entire global equation. A positive lag0 followed by a large negative lag3
means that high z5 three weeks ago now acts as a suppressant on z6. This 3-week
delayed negative feedback is consistent with the dengue intrinsic incubation
period in humans, which is typically 4 to 7 days, combined with the extrinsic
incubation period of 7 to 14 days, giving a total cycle of roughly 2 to 3 weeks
from infection to immune response. After a peak in transmission, immunity
accrues and transmission falls, which is exactly what a 3-week lag negative
coefficient would represent.

5.4 Level 2 cluster results in detail

Cluster 0 covers 3 provinces: Azua, San Juan, and San José de Ocoa. These are
rural inland western provinces. The cluster equation achieves a validation R2
of 0.7291 and a test R2 of 0.7386. The delta equation has 41 nonzero terms,
the second highest across all clusters. The large number of correction terms
with high magnitude coefficients indicates that the global equation fits these
provinces poorly and requires substantial adjustment. The ghost variable
candidates for this cluster are healthcare underreporting and geographic
isolation. Rural reporting systems in low-resource settings tend to capture
only a fraction of true cases, creating a systematic downward bias in observed
incidence that varies nonlinearly with actual transmission intensity.

Cluster 1 covers 6 provinces: Baoruco, Dajabón, Elías Piña, Independencia,
Santiago Rodríguez, and Valverde. All six provinces border or are very close
to the Haitian border. This cluster has 53 delta terms, the highest complexity
of all clusters, and achieves a validation R2 of 0.791 and test R2 of 0.7954.
The most scientifically important feature of this cluster is the presence of
nonzero constant bias terms in the delta equations for z1, z3, z4, z5, and z6.
The bias for delta_z1 is positive 0.163, for delta_z4 is positive 0.173, for
delta_z5 is negative 0.126, and for delta_z6 is positive 0.101. A constant
bias in a delta equation is the clearest possible mathematical evidence of an
unmeasured external forcing term. For border provinces, the most natural
candidate is cross-border disease importation from Haiti, a country with
higher dengue burden and substantially less vector control infrastructure.
This importation pressure acts as a constant additive term in the transmission
dynamics, elevating some latent dimensions and depressing others independently
of the current disease state.

Cluster 2 covers 5 provinces: El Seibo, La Altagracia, La Romana, Monte
Cristi, and Pedernales. This cluster achieves the best performance of all
clusters with validation R2 of 0.8267 and test R2 of 0.8188. It has only 21
delta terms, the fewest after Cluster 3. La Altagracia contains Punta Cana,
the largest tourism destination in the Caribbean. La Romana also has significant
tourism infrastructure. The ghost variable hypothesis for this cluster is that
high international tourist flows create a population mixing dynamic that
regularizes transmission, producing more predictable seasonal patterns that
the global model already captures well once trained on these provinces.

Cluster 3 covers 11 provinces: Distrito Nacional, Barahona, Espaillat, María
Trinidad Sánchez, Peravia, Puerto Plata, Samaná, San Cristóbal, San Pedro de
Macorís, Hato Mayor, and Santo Domingo. With 11 provinces this is the largest
cluster. It produces only 4 delta terms total, meaning the global equation
requires almost no correction for these provinces. Cluster 3 represents the
canonical Dominican dengue dynamic. These are predominantly coastal and
near-capital provinces with moderate to high urbanization, established public
health infrastructure, and moderate tourism activity. The near-zero delta for
this cluster means EpiHSD has essentially learned the canonical pattern from
this cluster and all other clusters are defined as deviations from it.

Cluster 4 covers 7 provinces: Duarte, La Vega, Hermanas Mirabal, Sánchez
Ramírez, Santiago, Monseñor Nouel, and Monte Plata. These are primarily
provinces of the Cibao Valley, the largest agricultural region in the country
and the center of sugarcane, rice, and vegetable production. The cluster achieves
validation R2 of 0.8055 and test R2 of 0.8087 with 34 delta terms. The ghost
variable candidate for the Cibao Valley is irrigation-driven vector habitat.
Agricultural irrigation creates permanent standing water sources that sustain
Aedes aegypti breeding year-round independent of rainfall, potentially modifying
the seasonal shape of transmission in ways the global model does not capture.

5.6 Why Azua is the hardest province

Azua consistently appears as the most difficult province to predict. It achieves
validation R2 of 0.7366 and test R2 of 0.6962. Azua is a small, extremely rural
province in the southwestern Dominican Republic. It has the lowest population
density of any province in Cluster 0, a poorly developed road network, and very
limited primary healthcare infrastructure. The likely explanation for its
difficulty is a combination of severe underreporting, meaning observed incidence
numbers are a highly noisy and biased proxy for true transmission, and high
dependence on unmeasured local factors such as water storage practices and
vector control. Despite being the worst province, EpiHSD still achieves a test R2
of 0.696 for Azua, which is an enormous improvement over all previous methods
that produced negative R2 for this province.

5.7 Why Santo Domingo is the easiest province

Santo Domingo achieves test R2 of 0.9149, meaning EpiHSD explains 91 percent
of the week-to-week variance in the latent trajectory of Santo Domingo. Santo
Domingo is the largest province by population, highly urbanized, adjacent to
the capital, with well-developed public health reporting systems and consistent
vector control programs. High reporting quality means the observed incidence is
a faithful proxy for true transmission. High urbanization means the mosquito
ecology is relatively uniform, dominated by urban container habitats, without
the complexity introduced by agricultural or rural settings. High vector control
consistency means the transmission dynamics follow predictable seasonal patterns
that the latent space and the EpiHSD equation can capture reliably.

5.8 The train to test generalization

One of the most important properties of these results is that the test R2 values
are close to or higher than the validation R2 values for most provinces. This
means EpiHSD is not overfitting to the validation period. The sparse regression
at each level explicitly controls the number of active parameters, and the
hierarchical residual structure limits the effective model complexity at each
level. The global equation has 10 to 14 active terms per output dimension out
of 25 possible terms. The cluster deltas are even sparser. The province deltas
are sparser still. This progressive sparsification prevents the total equation
from accumulating too many free parameters as it descends from global to cluster
to province.

5.9 The ghost variable finding

The most scientifically novel finding in the results is the structure of the
Cluster 1 delta equations. The constant bias terms in those equations are direct
quantitative evidence of a ghost variable affecting the border provinces. This
finding is completely inaccessible to any of the baseline methods. Random forest
cannot tell you that border provinces have a systematic offset in their latent
dynamics. GRU cannot tell you this either. Only a method that explicitly fits
interpretable residual equations in a hierarchical structure can reveal this
pattern. EpiHSD gives you not just a better R2 but a scientifically interpretable
finding that motivates a specific future data collection effort: measuring
cross-border population movement and case importation from Haiti. If that data
were added to the model as an additional input feature, the Cluster 1 constant
bias should shrink or disappear, confirming the ghost variable hypothesis.
