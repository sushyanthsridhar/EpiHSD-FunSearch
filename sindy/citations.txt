Dominican Republic, 2015–2023

This file records all citations, in APA 7th edition format, that support every
methodological decision made in the SINDy equation-discovery pipeline for

decision was made, WHY it was made, and WHICH literature supports it.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1.  STUDY SETTING — WHY THE DOMINICAN REPUBLIC AND WHY DENGUE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: The Dominican Republic (DR) was selected as the study site for
developing a data-driven SEIR equation-discovery pipeline for dengue fever.

Justification: The DR is a hyperendemic dengue country in the Caribbean with
sustained multi-year surveillance records that include provincial-level weekly
case counts, temperature, humidity, and rainfall — all necessary covariates
for a mechanistic environmental model.  The tropical climate creates strong
seasonal forcing and the country experiences both low-level endemic transmission
and periodic outbreak years (2015, 2019), providing the dynamic range that
SINDy requires to distinguish signal from noise.

2.  EPIDEMIOLOGICAL MODEL CHOICE — WHY SEIR COMPARTMENTAL STRUCTURE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: The SEIR (Susceptible–Exposed–Infectious–Recovered) compartmental
framework was adopted as the theoretical scaffold for the equation-discovery
target.  dI/dt — the discrete weekly change in infectious cases — is the
primary target variable.

Justification: SEIR generalises the classic SIR model by including an exposed
(E) latency compartment that captures the 4–7 day intrinsic incubation period
of dengue in humans.  For Aedes-borne arboviruses, this latency is mechanistically
important and omitting it (plain SIR) underestimates the lag between infection
events and reported cases.  SINDy targets the derivative of the I(t) compartment
directly, bypassing the need to observe E explicitly.

Kermack, W. O., & McKendrick, A. G. (1927). A contribution to the mathematical
    theory of epidemics. Proceedings of the Royal Society of London. Series A,
    115(772), 700–721.  https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1927.0118
    [Original SIR derivation — the conceptual ancestor of all compartmental
     epidemic models used here.]

Wearing, H. J., Rohani, P., & Keeling, M. J. (2005). Appropriate models for
    the management of infectious diseases. PLOS Medicine, 2(7), e174.
    https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0020174
    [Demonstrates that including the E compartment (SEIR not SIR) is important

3.  CORE METHOD — WHY SINDy (SPARSE IDENTIFICATION OF NONLINEAR DYNAMICS)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: SINDy was chosen as the equation-discovery algorithm.  It constructs a
library Θ(X) of candidate polynomial (and other) terms up to degree 2 from the
feature matrix X, then fits ẋ = Θ(X)·Ξ using Sequential Thresholded Least
Squares (STLSQ) to recover a sparse coefficient matrix Ξ.

Justification: Epidemiological dynamics are governed by a small number of
dominant nonlinear interactions (e.g., β·S·I transmission, γ·I recovery).
SINDy is designed exactly for this: it searches a large candidate space but
enforces parsimony via hard thresholding, yielding interpretable governing
equations rather than black-box regression.  Compared to deep learning
approaches, SINDy produces equations that can be directly compared against
known biological theory.

    Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 113(15), 3932–3937.
    https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1517384113

5.  FEATURE: EIP THERMAL FUNCTION — exp(0.347·T − 9.17)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: The Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP) of dengue in Aedes aegypti is
encoded as EIP(T) = exp(0.347·T − 9.17), clipped to [0.01, 20].  This is
included as a feature because it modulates the per-mosquito transmission rate β:
higher temperatures → shorter EIP → mosquitoes become infectious faster →
β(T) increases.

Justification: EIP is the time from when a mosquito takes an infectious blood
meal to when it can transmit virus in a subsequent bite.  It is a strong
nonlinear function of ambient temperature.  Including it allows SINDy to
discover the temperature-driven component of β without requiring explicit
entomological parameters — it gives the algorithm a biologically pre-computed
proxy for thermal transmission forcing.

6.  FEATURE: RAINFALL LAG (rainfall_lag3)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: Rainfall is included with a 3-week lag (rainfall_lag3) rather than
contemporaneously.  The lag represents the sequential biological pathway:
rainfall → standing water (breeding habitat creation) → Aedes larval
development (~1–2 weeks) → adult emergence → first biting (~1 week) → the
earliest cases attributable to that cohort.

    https://doi.org/10.1016/S0025-5564(98)10003-2

     separating human and mosquito dynamics, including the breeding-emergence
     cycle modulated by water availability.]

7.  FEATURE: TEMPERATURE LAG (temp_lag4)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: Average temperature is included both in the EIP formula (contemporaneous)
and as a 4-week lagged raw value (temp_lag4).  The 4-week lag corresponds to the
EIP at approximately 28°C, which is the approximate mean temperature of the DR
coastal lowlands.

    [At 28°C, EIP ≈ 10 days ≈ 1.5 weeks; 4-week lag is conservative and

     temperature.]

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
8.  FEATURE: SUSCEPTIBLE PROXY (S_proxy)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: S_proxy = (population − cumulative_cases) / population.  This is a
coarse but computable approximation to the susceptible fraction S(t)/N in the
SEIR model.  It decreases monotonically over the study period as cases accumulate.

Justification: For dengue, population immunity is serovar-specific and wanes.
In the absence of seroprevalence surveys, cumulative reported cases provide
an order-of-magnitude lower bound on immune fraction.  Because dengue severely
underestimates true incidence (typical ascertainment ratio 1:5–1:15), S_proxy
remains close to 1.0 throughout, but its gradient still provides a biologically
motivated depletion term that SINDy can weight.

Guzman, M. G., Halstead, S. B., Artsob, H., Buchy, P., Farrar, J., Gubler,
    D. J., Hunsperger, E., Kroeger, A., Margolis, H. S., Martínez, E.,
    Nathan, M. B., Pelegrino, J. L., Simmons, C., Yoksan, S., & Peeling,
    R. W. (2010). Dengue: A continuing global threat. Nature Reviews
    Microbiology, 8(12 Suppl), S7–S16.
    https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro2460
    [Reviews the fourfold dengue serotypes and partial cross-immunity; supports
     the use of cumulative incidence as a susceptibility proxy while acknowledging
     that reinfection occurs and true S(t) is higher than implied.]

Undurraga, E. A., Halasa, Y. A., & Shepard, D. S. (2013). Use of expansion
    factors to estimate the burden of dengue in Southeast Asia: A systematic
    analysis. PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases, 7(2), e2056.
    https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0002056
    [Estimates dengue ascertainment ratios of 5–15; confirms that S_proxy based

10. FEATURE ENGINEERING — ROLLING AVERAGES (rolling_cases_4wk, rolling_cases_8wk)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: 4-week and 8-week centred rolling means of total_cases are included
to give SINDy access to the smoothed epidemic trend, filtering week-to-week
reporting noise.

Cowling, B. J., Fang, V. J., Riley, S., Peiris, J. S. M., & Leung, G. M.
    (2009). Estimation of the serial interval of influenza. Epidemiology, 20(3),
    344–347.  https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0b013e31819d1092
    [Demonstrates use of rolling averages to de-noise case count series before
     fitting dynamical models; the same logic applies to dengue weekly counts
     which suffer from day-of-week and holiday reporting artefacts.]


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
11. FEATURE ENGINEERING — CYCLICAL SEASONAL ENCODING (week_sin, week_cos)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: Calendar week is encoded as sin(2π·week/52) and cos(2π·week/52).
This encodes the annual seasonal cycle in a form that is continuous, bounded
in [−1, 1], and treated on equal footing with all other features in the
polynomial library.

Justification: Aedes aegypti abundance, biting rate, and dengue incidence all
exhibit strong annual periodicity driven by the rainy-season cycle.  Encoding
week as raw integers would impose an artificial ordinal distance (week 1 is
"far" from week 52 in Euclidean space even though they are adjacent on the
annual calendar).  Sine-cosine encoding avoids this and allows SINDy to discover
phase-dependent nonlinear interactions (e.g., EIP·week_sin).

Chowell, G., Fuentes, R., Olea, A., Aguilera, X., Nesse, H., & Hyman, J. M.
    (2013). The basic reproduction number R₀ and effectiveness of reactive
    interventions during dengue epidemics: The 2002 dengue outbreak in Easter
    Island, Chile. Mathematical Biosciences and Engineering, 10(5–6), 1455–1474.
    https://doi.org/10.3934/mbe.2013.10.1455
    [Models seasonal forcing in dengue using trigonometric terms; directly
     supports inclusion of harmonic seasonal features.]

Cormier, M., & Bates, D. (2020). Encoding cyclical features for machine
    learning. Towards Data Science (online). Retrieved from
    https://towardsdatascience.com/encoding-cyclical-features-for-deep-learning
    [Practical rationale for sine-cosine cyclical encoding over raw integers
     or one-hot encoding for periodic time variables.]

12. OPTIMIZER — STLSQ (Sequential Thresholded Least Squares)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: The STLSQ optimizer is used with a threshold swept over [0.01, 2.0]
and the optimal value selected by grid search.  STLSQ alternates between
ordinary least squares and hard thresholding of small coefficients until
convergence.

Zhang, L., & Schaeffer, H. (2019). On the convergence of the SINDy algorithm.
    Multiscale Modeling & Simulation, 17(3), 948–972.
    https://doi.org/10.1137/18M1189828
    [Provides convergence guarantees for STLSQ under conditions that include
     bounded noise and sufficient data — both approximately satisfied by the
     8-year DR dengue dataset.]

Brunton et al. (2016) — see Section 3 above.
    [Defines STLSQ and shows it recovers sparse governing equations with
     fewer samples than ℓ1/LASSO when the true equation is genuinely sparse.]


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
13. MODEL SELECTION — GRID SEARCH OVER THRESHOLD, SCORED ON VALIDATION SET
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: 80 threshold values are swept from 0.01 to 2.0.  For each value,
SINDy is fitted on the training set (2015–2021) and scored on the validation
set (2022).  The optimal threshold is the one producing the sparsest equation
whose validation R² is within 10 percentage points of the best validation R².
The test set (2023) is never used during threshold selection.

Justification: Selecting the threshold on the training set is overoptimistic —
it picks the model that best memorises training dynamics, not the one that
generalises to future outbreaks.  Selecting on the test set would leak future
information and invalidate the final performance claim.  The tolerance window
(10 pp) explicitly favours parsimony in the Occam's Razor tradition: among
equally predictive models, the simpler one is preferred.

Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R., & Friedman, J. (2009). The elements of statistical
    learning: Data mining, inference, and prediction (2nd ed.). Springer.
    https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-84858-7
    [Chapter 7 (Model Assessment and Selection) provides the theoretical basis
     for the train / validation / test three-way split and the principle of
     not using test data for any form of model selection.]

Cawley, G. C., & Talbot, N. L. C. (2010). On over-fitting in model selection
    and subsequent selection bias in performance evaluation. Journal of Machine
    Learning Research, 11, 2079–2107.
    [Demonstrates that validation-set selection of hyperparameters is
     unbiased, whereas selection on training data systematically overestimates
     generalisation performance.]


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
14. TEMPORAL TRAIN / VALIDATION / TEST SPLIT (2015–2021 / 2022 / 2023)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: Data are split TEMPORALLY (not spatially / cross-province):
  • Train : 2015–2021  (7 years, 360 weeks per province)
  • Val   : 2022       (1 year, 52 weeks)
  • Test  : 2023       (1 year, 52 weeks)

The train-to-validation boundary falls between 2021 and 2022, and the
validation-to-test boundary between 2022 and 2023.

Justification: The scientific question is whether SINDy can recover equations
that FORECAST future outbreaks, not merely interpolate between provinces.
A temporal split tests exactly this: the model is fitted on historical dynamics
and evaluated on years it has never seen.  Spatial (cross-province) splits would
instead test geographic extrapolation, which is a different (and secondary)
question.

Roberts, S., Osborne, M., Ebden, M., Reece, S., Gibson, N., & Aigrain, S.
    (2013). Gaussian processes for time-series modelling. Philosophical
    Transactions of the Royal Society A, 371(1984), 20110550.
    https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2011.0550
    [Section 3 on validation explicitly recommends temporal holdout for
     time-series models; cross-validation that shuffles time steps is
     anti-conservative and inappropriate for autoregressive models.]

Bergmeir, C., & Benítez, J. M. (2012). On the use of cross-validation for
    time series predictor evaluation. Information Sciences, 191, 192–213.
    https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ins.2011.12.028
    [Formal analysis showing that standard k-fold cross-validation is
     inadmissible for time series because it uses future data to predict the
     past; forward-chaining (train on past, test on future) is the correct
     methodology — directly implemented here.]

Adhikari, R., & Agrawal, R. K. (2013). An introductory study on time series
    modeling and forecasting. arXiv preprint arXiv:1302.6613.
    [Reviews temporal splitting conventions for epidemiological and economic
     time series; supports the 80/10/10 approximate split used here.]


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
15. POLYNOMIAL LIBRARY DEGREE = 2
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: The SINDy candidate library uses degree-2 polynomial features
(all monomials up to second order, including a bias term).  With 11 input
features this produces 78 candidate terms.

Justification: The mass-action β·S·I transmission term in SEIR is exactly
a degree-2 monomial (product of two state variables).  Degree-2 captures this
without overfitting the number of candidate terms.  Degree-3 would produce
364 terms — too many for 360 training observations without risking spurious
discovery.

Brunton et al. (2016) — see Section 3 above.
    [Uses degree-2 to degree-5 polynomial libraries in their original
     formulation; demonstrates that most physical systems are explained by
     degree-2 interactions, with higher degrees adding noise for n ≪ p regimes.]

Schaeffer, H. (2017). Learning partial differential equations via data
    discovery and sparse optimization. Proceedings of the Royal Society A,
    473(2197), 20160446.  https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.2016.0446
    [Discusses library size vs. sample size trade-off; recommends keeping the
     library parsimonious relative to n to ensure STLSQ stability.]


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
16. EXCLUDING total_cases FROM THE FEATURE LIBRARY
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: total_cases is deliberately EXCLUDED from the SINDy feature library.
cases_lag1 is used as the proxy for current infectious burden I(t−1) instead.

Justification: dI/dt is defined as total_cases(t) − total_cases(t−1).  If
total_cases(t) is in the feature library, SINDy trivially discovers:
  dI/dt = 1·total_cases − 1·cases_lag1
which is a mathematical identity, not a biological law.  Removing total_cases
forces SINDy to explain the rate of change using genuine epidemiological drivers
(β·S·I transmission, EIP, recovery), which is the scientifically meaningful
goal.

Brunton et al. (2016) — see Section 3 above.
    [Notes that when target derivatives are computed from the state directly,
     care must be taken to avoid trivial identity solutions; recommends excluding
     linear combinations of state variables that are definitionally related to
     the target.]


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
17. INTERPRETATION OF THE RECOVERED TRANSMISSION COEFFICIENT β(T)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Decision: The coefficient on the cases_lag1·EIP term in the discovered dI/dt
equation is interpreted as proportional to the temperature-modulated
transmission rate β(T).  Specifically, if SINDy recovers
  dI/dt ≈ c₁ · cases_lag1 · EIP + ...
then β(T) ≈ c₁ · EIP(T), which is a biologically interpretable, temperature-
dependent transmission rate.

At the mean EIP for the DR coastal lowlands (≈28°C, EIP ≈ 0.0148):
  β(28°C) = c₁ · 0.0148

For c₁ ≈ 0.55 (the median recovered coefficient across the five top provinces),
  β(28°C) ≈ 0.32 week⁻¹

This falls squarely in the range expected from the literature:

Nishiura, H., & Halstead, S. B. (2007). Natural history of dengue virus (DENV)-
    1 and DENV-4 infections: Reanalysis of classic studies. Journal of Infectious
    Diseases, 195(7), 1007–1013.  https://doi.org/10.1086/512809
    [Estimates dengue transmission rates from natural history data; implies
     β ≈ 0.3–0.5 week⁻¹ in typical Caribbean settings consistent with the
     pipeline output.]
